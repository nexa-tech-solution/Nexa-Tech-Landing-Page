Most planning advice assumes that once something is written down, you will do it. With ADHD that is often where things fall apart: the list gets longer, the day slips away, and the plan becomes one more thing to feel bad about. A plan that works for an ADHD brain looks different. It is shorter, more visual and much more forgiving. Here is a simple way to build one.

These are everyday planning tips, not medical advice. If planning problems seriously affect your life, an ADHD specialist can help.

1. Empty your head first

Before planning, write down everything that is on your mind, from big tasks to "buy batteries". Do not sort it yet. Getting it out of your head lowers the background noise and stops you from planning around whatever you happen to remember.

2. Pick the essentials, not everything

From that list, choose the two or three things that really must happen today. Everything else is a bonus. On a low-energy day, finishing the essentials still counts as a good day. That matters, because a plan you fail every day is a plan you will stop using.

3. Put tasks on a timeline, not a list

A list hides how long things take. Place each task as a block on the day, with realistic time, and add the time for getting ready and travelling. You will see right away if the day does not fit, and it is better to find that out at 9 a.m. than at 9 p.m.

4. Make the first step tiny

Task paralysis usually comes from a step that feels too big. "Do taxes" is a project, "open the tax folder for two minutes" is a step. Break larger tasks down until the first step feels almost silly. Starting is the hard part, and momentum does the rest.

5. Make time visible

Many people with ADHD experience time blindness: an hour and ten minutes feel the same until the hour is gone. A visual countdown, where the remaining time shrinks in front of you, makes time something you can see instead of something you have to calculate.

6. Set leave-by reminders

A reminder at the start time is too late. Set reminders for when to start getting ready and when to leave, so being on time no longer depends on remembering.

7. Use routines for the repeat stuff

Mornings and evenings repeat every day. Turn them into short step-by-step routines so you do not have to plan them again each time. Keep them flexible. Skipping a step is fine, and nothing breaks if you miss a day.

8. Look back at what you did

At the end of the week, look at what you did get done rather than what is left. People with ADHD tend to remember the misses, and a quick look back balances that out.
